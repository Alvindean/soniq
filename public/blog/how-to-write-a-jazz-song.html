How to Write a Jazz Song: Structure, Hooks and the Moves That Work

How to write a jazz song: the structure, 120 BPM, line lengths, hook rules and the songwriting moves that make jazz work, with examples.

What a jazz song is made of

Improvisation over harmony, chord substitution, rhythmic sophistication, the conversation between instruments, space as music.

Jazz song structure

Head (melody) → Solos → Head out. Blues form (12-bar) or AABA (32-bar standard). The arrangement is a vehicle for improvisation.

Line length: how many syllables

The fastest way to make a chorus feel like a chorus is to make it shorter than the verse. These are the syllable ranges SONIQ targets for jazz:

  • Verse lines: 6–12 syllables
  • Pre-chorus lines: 5–8 syllables
  • Chorus lines: 5–9 syllables
  • Title / hook line: 3–6 syllables
  • Bridge lines: 5–9 syllables

The rules of jazz songwriting

  • The melody (head) is the launching pad — state it cleanly, then destroy it in the solo
  • Walking bass is always a counter-melody
  • Chord substitution creates harmonic surprise — ii-V-I can be approached from anywhere
  • Silence is the most important note in jazz

How the hit techniques play in jazz

SONIQ writes every song with the Hit Craft method: 22 techniques that separate a hit from a draft. Here is how the first eleven sound in jazz:

  • Syncopation ratio: Swing phrasing: 50%+ off-beat, anticipations on the 'and' of 4, lines that cross bar lines.
  • Density contrast: Scatted/dense bridge vs. sparse, held melody on the head.
  • Shadow rhythms: Repeat the head's rhythm with new words in the second A section (AABA form is shadow rhythm).
  • Dopamine triggering: Delay the resolving word over the turnaround; the witty last line of the A section.
  • Rhythmic displacement: Restate the title phrase starting on a different beat each A section.
  • The woman in the room: The torch-song angle: the whole lyric is spoken to one person across a smoky room.
  • Back phrasing: Classic behind-the-beat vocal jazz phrasing (Billie Holiday).
  • Micro-repetition: Scat fragments, 'you, you, you', repeated title words.
  • Scene sequencing: The bar, the rain on 52nd Street, the last train. Noir detail.
  • Repetition phrase types: AABA exact heads + incremental title + scat call-and-response + a tag ending.
  • Juicy lines: A witty, urbane line with an internal rhyme (Cole Porter energy).

Structure and sound in jazz: AABA: title in the first or last line of each A. The bridge is the unstable section. Witty perfect rhymes are the payoff.

Jazz substyles

  • Bebop: Scat density; heavy syncopation; displacement across fast changes.
  • Cool Jazz: Behind-the-beat cool; sparse density.
  • Hard Bop: Bluesy syncopation; call-and-response.
  • Modal Jazz: Long sustained phrasing; sparse density.
  • Free Jazz: Displacement everywhere; fragments and micro-repetition.
  • Jazz Fusion: Syncopated grooves; displacement across meter shifts.
  • Smooth Jazz: Relaxed back phrasing; romantic focus.
  • Vocal Jazz: Torch-song woman in the room; AABA shadow rhythm.
  • Big Band / Swing: Swing syncopation; call-and-response brass.
  • Gypsy Jazz: Fast syncopation; playful micro-repetition.
  • Soul Jazz / Acid Jazz: Groove syncopation; hooky micro-repetition.
  • Nu-Jazz: Electronic displacement; sparse vocals.

Songs that show how it's done

  • "Take Five" by Dave Brubeck: Odd time signature as identity — the "wrong" rhythm becomes the most natural thing after 4 bars
  • "So What" by Miles Davis: Space as expression — silence has emotional weight equal to sound
  • "Autumn Leaves" by Standard / Chet Baker: Chord substitution creates surprise and longing — the "wrong" note is the most right

Songs that broke the rules and won

  • "Take Five" — Dave Brubeck Quartet: 5/4 time signature — commercially unthinkable, programmers said listeners couldn't feel an odd meter. First jazz instrumental to sell over 1 million copies, proved listeners feel groove not math.
  • "Round Midnight" — Thelonious Monk: Extremely dissonant angular melody that sounded "wrong" to bebop musicians trained in smooth lines. Most recorded jazz standard written by a living composer — the wrongness WAS the emotional truth.
  • "A Love Supreme" — John Coltrane: Four-part spiritual suite with no traditional song structure, no hooks, no commercial concessions. Best-selling jazz album of all time — proved jazz listeners wanted transcendence more than entertainment.

The second voice: Walking bass (primary counter) / piano comping / horn counterpoint

Jazz has the richest counter-melody tradition: the walking bass is ALWAYS a counter-melody — it creates an independent melodic line of its own against the soloist. Piano comping creates harmonic counter-commentary. In ensemble sections, horns play genuine counterpoint — multiple independent melodic lines simultaneously, each complete on its own.

Where it goes: Throughout: walking bass provides constant counter / Head: piano comps creates harmonic counter / Solos: bass and comping in three-voice counterpoint / Ensemble: full horn counterpoint

Ad-libs and vocables

scat syllables: doo-wah, bop, skee-dat, ba-da, used improvised solo sections, outro scat fade. Jazz vocables (scat) are melodically sophisticated — they mimic instrument lines, not crowd participation

FAQ

What is the structure of a jazz song?

Head (melody) → Solos → Head out. Blues form (12-bar) or AABA (32-bar standard). The arrangement is a vehicle for improvisation.

What tempo is jazz music?

Most jazz songs sit around 120 BPM. Improvisation over harmony, chord substitution, rhythmic sophistication, the conversation between instruments, space as music.

How long should jazz lyric lines be?

Verse lines usually run 6–12 syllables, chorus lines 5–9, and the title or hook line 3–6. Keeping the chorus clearly shorter than the verse is what makes it easy to sing back.

What makes a good jazz hook?

AABA exact heads + incremental title + scat call-and-response + a tag ending. A witty, urbane line with an internal rhyme (Cole Porter energy).
